Project Statement: Unmasking Our Algorithm

In her seminal work Unmasking AI, Dr. Joy Buolamwini argues that 'the data we use to train systems is a reflection of our history and our priorities. For this assignment, our group wanted to move beyond the convenience of web-scraped, non-consensual massive datasets by constructing an Intentional Corpus of 300 original images. Our project classifies three distinct biological categories: Domesticated Animals, Agricultural Produce, and the Human Body.

Team Members: Grace Bonjour, Vincent Frey, Vivek Saravanan

Resonance with 'Unmasking AI'

As we completed this project, Dr. Joy Buolamiwini's concepts, like the "coded gaze", became extremely relevant to our work process. Buolamiwini defines the coded gaze as the embedded priorities and agendas that are used by people in power to shape our technology. In our experiment, we noticed how we were unintentionally representing the "gaze" that Buloamiwini mentioned. When we decided what would qualify as a 'dog' or a 'fruit', we were creating a label, or rather a micro-standard for our perceived world. This idea became even more apparent when we conducted our testing phase. For example, if the model failed to recognize a peeled fruit compared to an unpeeled fruit, it mirrored the exclusion that Buolamwini encountered with facial recognition software. This example of a peeled fruit can be seen as the metaphorical 'dark skin', where the training data doesn't contain much information about a certain race, or in our case, peeled vs unpeeled fruits. Determining accuracy is a subjective metric, meaning our model is only 100% accurate within the controlled and narrow environment we created for it. When other factors like different lighting, varied angles, and different backgrounds came into play, the machine began having trouble.

Ethics of Data Collection

In Unmasking AI, a theme that stood out to us was the 'Data Wealth' gap and the fundamental exploitative nature of data scraping. Large companies often grow in power by harvesting, often without consent, millions of images from the internet. The creators or owners of these images are usually unaware that their pictures are being used for AI training and are rarely compensated for this involuntary participation. Unlike the large-scale datasets discussed in Unmasking AI that often use non-consensual web scraping, our dataset was built on active, intentional contribution. Every image in our 300+ sample set was captured with full awareness of the context and the creator(us). Compared to the millions of images scraped by large AI companies, 100 images per object was very little. It raised the question of whether a small-scale, ethical data set is better than a large-scale, non-consensual, biased data set. The reality is, the power of data and code should not come at the expense of human agency. By documenting our data set and making it easily available via GitHub, we are supporting the 'transparent Gaze', where the limitations of our data are easily as apparent as our results.

The Technical Process

For this experiment, we gathered a corpus of 300 original images. Each group member contributed 100 unique samples to ensure our training data was robust.

Working on the model itself was an iterative process. After uploading our three class folders into Google's Teachable Machine, we trained and stress-tested the classifier against images outside of our original corpus. We tweaked the balance of samples between classes and reran the training loop until the live predictions felt stable. We finally exported the trained model and wired it into this page with p5.js and ml5.
