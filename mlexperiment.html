Project Statement: Unmasking Our Algorithm

In her seminal work Unmasking AI, Dr. Joy Buolamwini argues that 'the data we use to train systems is a reflection of our history and our priorities. For this assignment, our group wanted to move beyond the convenience of web-scraped, non-consensual massive datasets by constructing an Intentional Corpus of 300 original images. Our project classifies three distinct biological categories: Domesticated Animals, Agricultural Produce, and the Human Body.

Team Members: Grace Bonjour, Vincent Frey, Vivek Saravanan

Resonance with 'Unmasking AI'

As we completed this project, Dr. Joy Buolamiwini's concepts, like the "coded gaze", became extremely relevant to our work process. Buolamiwini defines the coded gaze as the embedded priorities and agendas that are used by people in power to shape our technology. In our experiment, we noticed how we were unintentionally representing the "gaze" that Buloamiwini mentioned. When we decided what would qualify as a 'dog' or a 'fruit', we were creating a label, or rather a micro-standard for our perceived world. This idea became even more apparent when we conducted our testing phase. For example, if the model failed to recognize a peeled fruit compared to an unpeeled fruit, it mirrored the exclusion that Buolamwini encountered with facial recognition software. This example of a peeled fruit can be seen as the metaphorical 'dark skin', where the training data doesn't contain much information about a certain race, or in our case, peeled vs unpeeled fruits. Determining accuracy is a subjective metric, meaning our model is only 100% accurate within the controlled and narrow environment we created for it. When other factors like different lighting, varied angles, and different backgrounds came into play, the machine began having trouble.

Ethics of Data Collection

In Unmasking AI, a theme that stood out to us was the 'Data Wealth' gap and the fundamental exploitative nature of data scraping. Large companies often grow in power by harvesting, often without consent, millions of images from the internet. The creators or owners of these images are usually unaware that their pictures are being used for AI training and are rarely compensated for this involuntary participation. Unlike the large-scale datasets discussed in Unmasking AI that often use non-consensual web scraping, our dataset was built on active, intentional contribution. Every image in our 300+ sample set was captured with full awareness of the context and the creator(us). Compared to the millions of images scraped by large AI companies, 100 images per object was very little. It raised the question of whether a small-scale, ethical data set is better than a large-scale, non-consensual, biased data set. The reality is, the power of data and code should not come at the expense of human agency. By documenting our data set and making it easily available via GitHub, we are supporting the 'transparent Gaze', where the limitations of our data are easily as apparent as our results.

The Technical Process

The first step of our project was data collection. This was one of the most important components, and was crucial to the model's performance. Each group member contributed 100 original samples, resulting in a dataset of around 300 images. The requirement to provide our own images made us think about what original data means and it can affect our model. Things like lighting, backgrounds, and camera angles needed to be varied to ensure that our model can identify the correct object in different scenarios. At first we thought that more data would lead to better results, but it became apparent that the quality and diversity of the data was just as important. The varied settings of the images we used were important to ensure that our model would be able to classify objects accurately in multiple settings. Not having data which is diverse can potentially lead to biases and inaccuracies in the model, which is why it is so important.

After creating our dataset, we used Teachable Machine to train our model. The process of training a model was simple to the user, since we just had to upload the pictures and provide labels. Behind the scenes, however, there was much more going on. The training process involved tuning weights in a neural network to capture patterns in the images based on the labels. This is another reason why diverse data was crucial, we want to ensure that the model is capturing what we actually want to capture, not some patterns in the background or setting. When testing the model, it was pretty accurate at classifying our inputs, but it did occasionally make mistakes. These mistakes were most apparent when we provided the model with situations that were unfamiliar to it. These errors show the limitations of these systems and how important it is to understand how and why they work.

One important thing we learned from this process was much the data can impact the machine learning models. Even minor discrepancies or imbalances in the dataset could lead to big differences in performance, or even cause the model to be more biased. This connects to some of the themes in Unmasking AI, like how biased or incomplete datasets can lead to biased outcomes. This project demonstrated how easily these issues can arise, and how important it is that the most used and widespread models also address these issues.

This experience gave us a firsthand understanding of how easily bias can be introduced during the data collection and labeling stages. When we chose which objects qualified as a "fruit" or which dog images were most representative of its category, we were making subjective decisions that directly shaped our model's behavior. These small, human-driven choices echo the much larger-scale decisions made by scientists at major AI companies, where the stakes are far higher and the impact on marginalized communities is well-documented. Our project, though small in scale, reinforced that intentionality at every step of the pipeline is essential for building more fair and accountable systems.

Conclusion

Our project served as a microcosm of the broader challenges Dr. Joy Buolamwini addresses in Unmasking AI. By deliberately constructing our own dataset and confronting the ethical and technical complexities of machine learning firsthand, we gained a deeper appreciation for the responsibility that comes with building AI systems. The coded gaze is not an abstract concept reserved for large-scale commercial models, it operates at every level, including our own small-scale experiments. Moving forward, we believe that ethical awareness, transparent data practices, and a commitment to diverse representation should be foundational to how all AI systems are designed, regardless of their scale.
